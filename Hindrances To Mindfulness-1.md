# The Five Hindrances (Nivarana): A Guide to Mindfulness Obstacles

In mindfulness, obstacles are not "failures" or moral lapses; they are natural mental states that arise when the mind is untrained. Monks often compare the mind to a bowl of water: if the water is disturbed, you cannot see your reflection clearly. These disturbances are categorized into the Five Hindrances, plus additional subtle obstacles that modern practitioners often face.

## 1. Sensory Desire & Attachment (Kamachanda) - Including Lust

**The Complaint:** "I feel a pull toward pleasure—thinking about food, entertainment, or physical comfort—that makes the present moment feel 'not enough.' Specifically, I am caught in **Lust**, a powerful physical craving for another person."

**The Monk's Analogy:**

> "Sensory desire is like **water mixed with colorful dye**. It looks attractive, but it obscures the clarity of the water. Lust is like **being wounded and scratching the itch**; the man with a skin disease scratches the wound for a moment of relief, but the scratching actually makes the infection worse. Seeking the object of lust is the 'scratching'—it feels good for a second, but it keeps the 'wound' of craving raw and unhealed."

### What to do:

1. **The "Disenchantment" Review**
    
    - **How to do it:** When a desire arises, mentally fast-forward the clock. If you desire a luxury meal, visualize the moment it is finished, the dirty dishes, and the feeling of a heavy stomach. If you desire an object, visualize it ten years from now, covered in dust in a junkyard.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** Monks teach that desire is a **"mirage of the desert."** From a distance, the sand looks like cool water. As you run toward it, the water remains "over there." By reviewing the end-state (disenchantment), you walk up to the mirage and see it is just sand. You stop running, which is the only way to find true rest.
        
2. **The "Pause and Pulse" Method**
    
    - **How to do it:** Stop the mental story and drop into the body. Locate the "craving" physically. Is it a vibration in the solar plexus? A heat in the face? Breath _into_ the center of that sensation. Watch it like a scientist watching a laboratory specimen. Note how the sensation grows, peaks, and inevitably fades without you doing anything.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** Desire is like **"a fire that needs wood."** The "wood" is your attention and the mental images you feed it. By staying with the raw physical pulse and refusing to provide mental images, you stop adding wood. The fire has no choice but to go out on its own.
        
3. **Lust-Specific: Biological Realism (Asubha)**
    
    - **How to do it:** Mentally peel back the "skin" of the person you desire. Systematically visualize the 32 parts of the body: the bile, the phlegm, the fat, the sweat, the intestines, and the excrement. Imagine the skeleton beneath the face.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** We are like **"people fooled by a painted pot full of filth."** We see the beautiful paint (the skin/makeup) and forget what is inside. Looking at the organs is not about being "grossed out" for the sake of it; it is about seeing the **"truth of the vessel."** When you see the pot is full of bile and blood, the "intoxication" of lust vanishes because the illusion of beauty is revealed as a temporary surface tension.
        

## 2. Ill-Will & Aversion (Vyapada) - Including Hate & Jealousy

**The Complaint:** "I feel irritation or judgment. I am burning with **Hate** toward someone who wronged me, or **Jealousy** because someone else has the success I want."

**The Monk's Analogy:**

> "Ill-will is like **boiling water**. Bubbling and hissing, it is dangerous to touch and impossible to see through. **Hate** is like **grasping a hot coal** with the intent of throwing it at someone else; you are the one who gets burned. **Jealousy** is like **drinking poison and expecting the other person to die**; it is a termite that slowly eats your own internal structure."

### What to do:

1. **Hate-Specific: The "Inheritor of Actions"**
    
    - **How to do it:** Recite: "This person is the owner of their actions. I am the owner of my actions." Visualize the person's anger as a heavy, ugly gift they are trying to hand you. If you get angry back, you have "accepted the gift." If you remain mindful, the gift stays with them.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** Anger is like **"spitting into the wind."** The spit only comes back onto your own face. By realizing that their bad behavior will naturally bring them suffering (Kamma), you no longer feel the need to "punish" them. You realize they are already walking into a fire they built themselves.
        
2. **Jealousy-Specific: Appreciative Joy (Mudita)**
    
    - **How to do it:** When you see someone succeed, catch the "pang" in your chest. Immediately "pivot" the thought: "There is so much suffering in the world; I am glad at least one person is having a good day." Imagine their happiness as a light that makes the whole world a bit brighter for everyone, including you.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** Jealousy is like **"being a beggar who hates anyone with a coin."** It keeps you poor. Appreciative joy is like **"finding a gold mine in your neighbor's yard and realizing the whole village is now richer."** It allows you to enjoy a success that you didn't even have to work for.
        
3. **The "Softening" Breath**
    
    - **How to do it:** Locate the "tight knot" of aversion in the body (usually the gut or chest). On the inhale, acknowledge the knot ("I see you, anger"). On the exhale, use the word "Soft" or "Let go" and imagine the muscle fibers relaxing like a rubber band being released.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** Aversion is **"mental rigour mortis."** You become hard and brittle. By softening the breath, you become like **"water flowing around a rock."** The rock (the problem) stays there, but the water (you) is no longer bruised by it.
        

## 3. Sloth, Torpor & Mental Fog (Thina-middha)

**The Complaint:** "My mind is heavy, dull, or sleepy. I lack the clarity to focus on anything."

**The Monk's Analogy:**

> "This is like **water covered in thick algae**. It is also like **being in a prison cell** where the walls are made of thick blankets—it's comfortable, but you are not free."

### What to do:

1. **Forceful Oxygenation (Bellows Breath)**
    
    - **How to do it:** Sit up very straight. Imagine a string pulling the crown of your head to the ceiling. Take 20 rapid, shallow breaths through the nose—pump the belly like a bellows. Then, take one massive inhale, hold for 5 seconds, and let it out slowly.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** Sloth is like **"a stagnant pond."** Mosquitoes and disease breed there. This breath is like **"throwing a large stone into the pond."** It creates ripples and splashes, forcing the stagnant energy to move and the "algae" (fog) to break apart.
        
2. **Mental Brightening (The Halogen Bulb)**
    
    - **How to do it:** Close your eyes and imagine a point of light at the center of the eyebrows. Make it grow until it is a blindingly white sun. If you feel "heavy," imagine the top of your skull opening up and the light of the galaxy pouring in.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** The mind is like **"a room with the curtains drawn."** Sloth is simply the absence of light. You don't need to fight the darkness; you just need to **"turn on the lamp."** By visualizing light, you signal the "Sun-mind" to arise.
        

## 4. Restlessness & Worry (Uddhacca-kukucca)

**The Complaint:** "My mind is racing. I feel 'wired but tired.' I am replaying mistakes or worrying about the future."

**The Monk's Analogy:**

> "Restlessness is like **water whipped by a high wind**. It is like **being a slave** to a master who never lets you sit down."

### What to do:

1. **The "Vast Sky" Meditation**
    
    - **How to do it:** Expand your field of awareness. Instead of focusing on just the tip of the nose, feel the air against your skin, the sounds in the distance, and the space in the room. Treat thoughts like tiny mosquitoes flying in a giant cathedral—they have so much space that they don't bother you.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** Restlessness is like **"a wild bull caught in a tiny pen."** It will break the fence. If you put the bull in a **"vast open meadow,"** it can run as much as it wants without hitting anything. Eventually, it gets tired and lies down on its own. Don't try to tie the bull; just give it more room.
        
2. **Counting the Breath (The Anchor)**
    
    - **How to do it:** Count "One" at the end of the first exhale. "Two" at the end of the second. If a thought interrupts, start back at "One." Be extremely gentle. The goal is not to reach ten; the goal is to _notice_ when you've wandered.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** The mind is like **"a drunken monkey."** Counting the breath is like **"giving the monkey a shiny stick to play with."** It keeps the monkey's hands busy so it stops pulling your hair and breaking the furniture.
        

## 5. Doubt & Indecision (Vicikiccha)

**The Complaint:** "Is this working? Am I a bad meditator? Maybe I should try a different app."

**The Monk's Analogy:**

> "Doubt is like **standing at a crossroads in a dark forest**. It is like **muddy water**; you can't tell if there is treasure or a snake at the bottom."

### What to do:

1. **The "Experimenter" Mindset**
    
    - **How to do it:** Set a timer for 15 minutes. Make a vow: "For these 15 minutes, I am a scientist. I will not judge the experiment until it is finished." If a doubt arises ("This is boring"), label it "Doubt, doubt" and return to the breath.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** Doubt is like **"digging a well."** You dig 2 feet, don't find water, and move to a new spot. You dig 2 feet there, no water, move again. After a day, you have ten holes but no water. If you just **"dig in one place"** deeply enough, the water will eventually gush out.
        
2. **Recollection of Teachers**
    
    - **How to do it:** Think of someone you respect who is calm and wise. Realize they sat through this exact same doubt. Remind yourself that millions have used this "map" for 2,500 years.
        
    - **The Monk's Explanation:** You are like **"a traveler who doesn't trust the map."** You keep stopping to check the compass. By remembering that others have reached the city using this exact road, you stop doubting the map and **"just keep walking."**
